## Symptoms
- Upward curling and cupping of leaflets, yellowing of leaf margins, small crumpled leaves.
- Stunted plants with bushy, shortened internodes.
- Flowers drop; very few fruits form. **Plants infected early may produce almost no yield.**
- Symptoms appear about 2-3 weeks after infection by whitefly.

## Key facts
- There is **no chemical cure for infected plants**; sprays only kill the whitefly vector, not the virus in the plant.
- Spreads by whiteflies, not by seed, soil or touch (in practice). The virus persists in weeds and other crops (e.g., cotton, okra, chilli, papaya, some weeds).
- Infection in the first 50-60 days after transplanting causes the greatest yield loss.

## Favorable conditions
Warm, dry weather with high whitefly populations; plantings near cotton, okra, or older infected tomato fields.

## Prevention (most important)
- Raise seedlings in **insect-proof nets (40-50 mesh)** nurseries; keep seedlings covered until transplant.
- Use **resistant/tolerant hybrids** (Ty-gene carrying) suited to the region.
- Do not transplant seedlings with any curling or yellowing.
- Install **yellow sticky traps** in nursery and field to monitor whiteflies.
- Use border crops (e.g., maize or sorghum) to reduce whitefly entry; UV-reflective (silver) mulch repels whiteflies.
- Remove weeds and volunteer plants; avoid staggered planting next to older infected fields.
- Maintain a tomato-free period (crop-free interval) between seasons where possible.

## What to do when infected plants are seen
- **Uproot and destroy (bag, bury or burn) infected plants** immediately (roguing), particularly early in the season.
- Control whiteflies in the surrounding plants so they do not spread the virus to healthy plants.
- Do not use fruit from the plant as seed source.

## Whitefly management (organic / biological)
- Yellow sticky traps, neem oil or neem seed kernel extract, soap/horticultural oil, and biological agents such as Beauveria bassiana, Lecanicillium (Verticillium) lecanii.
- Conserve natural enemies (e.g., Encarsia, Eretmocerus parasitoids).

## Whitefly management (chemical; verify on CIB&RC; follow label dose, interval and PHI)
- Nursery/early stage systemic options where registered: imidacloprid, thiamethoxam (IRAC 4A).
- Other options rotate by IRAC group: diafenthiuron (12A), pyriproxyfen (7C), spiromesifen (23), acetamiprid (4A), and others per state recommendation.
- Rotate groups, avoid repeated neonicotinoid use (whitefly resistance is common), and apply as part of an integrated program, not alone.

## Stage-wise action
| Stage | Risk | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Nursery | Very high | Net-covered nursery; systemic protection per recommendation; sticky traps |
| Transplanting | Very high | Plant only healthy seedlings; reflective mulch; border crop |
| Vegetative | High | Scout weekly; rogue infected plants immediately; whitefly control |
| Flowering/fruit set | Medium | Continue whitefly management; roguing is less effective, and late infections cause smaller losses |
| Fruit development | Low-Medium | Manage whiteflies to protect against spread to neighbouring plantings |
| Ripening/harvest | Low | Remove old crop quickly after final harvest |
| End of season | - | Destroy crop residues; remove weeds; avoid overlapping crops |

## Look-alikes
Herbicide (2,4-D) injury, nutrient deficiency, and physiological leaf roll can resemble leaf curl. Check for whiteflies and the pattern of stunting and yellow margins. Tomato mosaic virus gives mottling and distortion rather than upward curl.
